SAT - Think of SAT like holding up a flashlight in a dark room. If you shine a light on two objects, they cast shadows onto the wall behind them.
If their shadows overlap from EVERY single angle, it means the objects are physically crashing into each other.
If there is even ONE angle where the shadows do not touch, it means there is a gap of empty space between them, and NO collision has occurred.


Without this exact vector math, if a player runs fast enough toward a wall, they will clip inside it and get permanently trapped. 
SAT gives us the exact mathematical data needed to smoothly push the player back outside the wall on every single frame.

By making our moving player a circle, we give our engine a mathematical superpower: Invariance to Rotation.

A circle looks identical from every single angle.
Whether your player rotates left, spins right, or moves diagonally, the distance from the center to the outer edge is always exactly the same: the radius.




Step 14: Laying the Geometry Foundation for Separating Axis Theorem (SAT).
-The Separating Axis Theorem (SAT) cannot understand simple rectangular data strings like (x, y, width, height). Instead, it treats every object in your game like a solid polygon geometric shape.
 To use SAT, our code must know the explicit positions of the Vertices (the exact x and y coordinates of all 4 corners) for every single room.

-The Separating Axis Theorem works by projecting the shadows of your game objects onto different angular lines (axes) to check if those shadows overlap. If there is a gap between the shadows, it means no collision is happening!

-To calculate those projection shadow lines accurately, the math formulas must know where the corner vertices sit. By pre-calculating these four points for each room during setup, 
we keep the game engine optimized and ready to handle fast collision lookups 60 times per second.

Step 18: Flattening a Wall Box onto a 1D Axis

Unlike our smooth player circle, a wall box's shadow changes size depending on the angle we look at it from. If it is turned slightly, its corners stick out differently!

To safely calculate its 1D shadow span, we have to:
Grab all 4 absolute corner points of the wall.

Track which corner landed the furthest to the left (our absolute min) and which corner landed the furthest to the right - our absolute max

Step 19: Detecting the Shadow Overlap - 
We are writing a small mathematical evaluator function called darkSpaceOverlap_check.
This function takes two separate shadow spans—the player's shadow range and the wall box's shadow range—and
 calculates whether they are currently occupying the same space on our measuring axis line.

According to the Separating Axis Theorem (SAT), two shapes are colliding if, and only if, their shadows overlap on every single axis we test. 
If we find even one axis where the shadows don't touch, a gap exists, meaning there is absolutely no collision.

Step 20: assembling our collision function

We are writing the final, core collision function called darkSpaceCollision_circleWithBox.

This function gathers the unique testing directional lines (axes) required to check a circle against a box, 
loops through them using a loop, and runs our shadow math on every single one. If all axes overlap, it tells the engine a collision is happening.

To thoroughly prove a round player circle is colliding with a rigid box wall, we have to look for gaps from a few strategic angles:

Horizontal Viewpoint: Look directly down the X-axis ({ x: 1, y: 0 }) to see if they overlap left-to-right.

Vertical Viewpoint: Look directly down the Y-axis ({ x: 0, y: 1 }) to see if they overlap top-to-bottom.

The Corner Viewpoint: Look along a custom diagonal vector pointing from the box's closest corner straight toward the center of the circle. 
This makes sure our engine handles rounded corner slides perfectly!

If our engine scans through those viewpoints and finds even a single gap, it exits early and returns false. 
If it finishes checking all views and finds zero gaps, it safely guarantees a collision is occurring.
